Can LLMs Hear the Machine?

A blind test of whether ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude can tell AI-generated music from human music, run as a difficulty ladder. Innovation team · Atharva · run 2 · 16 October 2026

Where we are. The pilot (12 Oct) tested only the hardest condition, and every model was at chance. Run 2 starts from the easiest condition, the untouched file, and removes giveaways step by step. Run 2 is complete for three levels: 90 chats (3 levels × 3 models × 10 tracks, two turns each). Levels L1 and L3 are designed but not run yet.

1. The question

People already upload songs to chat assistants and ask "is this AI?". We want to know whether the answers mean anything: can the model detect AI music, is its confidence honest, and what is it actually basing the verdict on?

Ten tracks, as five style-matched pairs: 5 made by Suno v5 / v5.5 (2026) and 5 human tracks from Creative Commons netlabels and ccMixter, all released before 2022 (lo-fi chillhop, UK garage / house-pop, jazzy trap, afro house, dub techno). These are deliberately hard pairs: the project owner can't reliably tell them apart by ear either.

2. The difficulty ladder

Each level removes giveaways from the level above. Detection that works on L0 but fails on L4 tells you what the model was relying on.

Note on the human files: they are actually louder than the Suno files (−7.5 to −11.7 LUFS against about −14), so a model that reasons "loud and compressed means AI" would get L2 backwards.

3. How each test works (with an example)

Every file goes into a fresh chat (temporary, or memory off), and the chat has two turns.

Turn 1, describe. AI is not mentioned. This checks whether the model can perceive the file at all. It asks for tempo, key, instruments, vocals, duration, sample rate, bitrate, embedded tags, loudness and frequency ceiling, plus how the model accessed the audio ("listened directly", "code analysis only", …).

Turn 2, verdict. In the same chat, the model is told the file is either AI-generated or human-made and asked for a JSON verdict, a confidence of 50–100, whether it recognised the song, the main basis for the verdict and up to five pieces of evidence.

Example: one Suno track (afro house, 4 min 8 s) as a raw file (L0)

How to read it. Brier is the mean squared error of the stated probability. 0.25 is what you get by always answering 50%, so anything near or above 0.25 is no better than not answering. AUROC asks whether AI tracks got higher "AI" probabilities than human ones (0.5 = chance). With 10 tracks per cell every number has wide error bars.

What happens at each step

  • L0 → L2 (tags and format removed): ChatGPT and Claude lose their only reliable evidence. ChatGPT drops from 10/10 to 4/10 and says "AI" for 9 of 10 files. Claude drops to 7/10. Two of its correct "human" calls cite the track length ("9:29 long, longer than a typical generation"), which is a real leak at L2.
  • L2 → L4 (cut to 30 s, loudness-matched): ChatGPT says "AI" for all 10 clips (5/10). Claude says "AI" for 9 of 10 (6/10). Neither moves meaningfully.
  • Gemini barely changes across levels (6/10 everywhere), as expected for a model that never reads the file's metadata. It leans "HUMAN" and justifies it with generic phrases ("clean transients", "natural groove").
  • Claude's AUROC of 0.88–0.90 at L2/L4 means its confidence was slightly higher on AI tracks than on human ones. Its confidence only ranged from 52% to 62%, though, so this is a faint ranking signal on 10 tracks, not usable detection.

How these were estimated. The consumer apps don't show token counts, so these are reconstructed from the text on the page (about 4 characters per token). They cover the prompts, both answers and, for ChatGPT, the code it ran and printed. Turn 2 re-sends the whole conversation, so its input includes Turn 1.

  • Gemini processes the audio itself. Audio is counted at Gemini's published API rate of 32 tokens per second, sent with both turns. That is why full tracks cost far more than clips.
  • ChatGPT and Claude never put the audio into the model. It sits in a code sandbox, so it costs no tokens. Claude hides its tool calls in the page, so its figures are lower bounds. Hidden reasoning tokens are not counted for anyone.
  • Cost is illustrative only: tokens priced at $3 per million input and $15 per million output, one rate for all three, not checked against any provider's current price list. At that rate, 1,000 files would cost roughly $45 (ChatGPT), $13+ (Claude) or $13–$60 (Gemini, clip vs full track). A real figure needs the same test run through each provider's API, which logs tokens per request.

Token estimates come from 50 of the 90 chats (all of L2, plus L4 for ChatGPT and Gemini). Length capture was added after L0 and Claude's L4 had run. The runs themselves cost nothing beyond the existing subscriptions.

6. What this means

  1. "Detection" by ChatGPT and Claude is metadata reading. Given the raw file, both open it with code, find made with suno or a 2009–2019 artist tag, and answer with 90–99% confidence. Once the tags are gone they drop to chance (4–7/10) and mostly default to "AI".
  2. Claude is honest about it. In nearly every answer it says it cannot listen and that its verdict rests on tags or weak technical cues. Its confidence stays at 52–62% when it has nothing, which is well calibrated.
  3. ChatGPT sounds as if it listened, but its answers don't support that. On clean files it writes about "repetitive motifs" and "polished synth timbres" and says "AI" almost every time, including on human tracks. At L2 it called a human jazzy-trap beat "cinematic orchestral".
  4. Gemini is the only model that listens, and listening alone didn't work. It got 6/10 at every level, misses most Suno tracks with 75–92% confidence, gets basic facts like track length wrong, and sometimes can't process a file at all.
  5. Our own cleanup leaks into the evidence. At L4, Claude repeatedly cites the 15 kHz cutoff and the fade that our sanitiser adds to every file. At L2, with no cap, it used track length instead. These cues are identical for AI and human files, or only weakly related to them, so they can't support real detection.
  6. Practical takeaway: "Is this AI?" answers from chat assistants are only reliable when the file still carries its provenance tags, and then a simple tag reader would do the same job. On clean audio, none of these assistants detects AI music better than chance.

7. Caveats

  • 10 tracks and one run per item. This is a smoke test, not a measurement.
  • Gemini's app now shows "Gemini 4" with the mode picker on Flash. That differs from the 3.8 Flash used in the pilot, so Gemini isn't directly comparable across runs.
  • Some chats were redone after technical failures: a frozen tab, a misrouted upload, and a ChatGPT upload that failed twice. One Claude chat was resumed after a pause. Gemini's "could not access" answers are scored as given (50%). One-off retries, which failed again, are in results/ladder_retries.jsonl.
  • Human tracks were chosen from metadata; vocal and instrumental labels haven't been checked by ear.

8. Next steps

  1. Drop the 15 kHz low-pass and fades from the clip level, so the models' "evidence" isn't our own processing. Optionally run L1 (tags stripped only) to see whether 48 kHz and encoder fingerprints alone are enough for the code-using models.
  2. Move to API runs with native-audio models for real listening tests and exact token costs.
  3. Scale up from 10 to 40+ tracks with 3 runs per item before ranking any model.
  4. Check Gemini's "could not access" failures, which clustered on Suno files.
